Lesson 6 · Keep AI Honest, and Know What It Costs

Using AI well for the long run means staying honest about how you use it, and clear-eyed about what it actually costs you.

What you'll learn

  • When to disclose you used AI, and why hallucinations happen in the first place
  • How different AI models actually compare, so you stop using one tool for every job
  • How to figure out whether your AI subscription is actually worth what you pay for it

Concept

Three things worth being clear-eyed about

Honesty: when to disclose, and why hallucinations happen

Disclose AI use whenever the output feeds into someone else's decision: a work deliverable, a grade, a claim you're presenting as fact. Hallucinations aren't a rare glitch. The model is predicting plausible-sounding text, not checking it against a source, so a confident wrong answer is a normal failure mode, not an edge case.

Cost: models are not interchangeable

Models differ in speed, depth of reasoning, price per use, and what they're built for. A fast, cheap model is fine for a quick rewrite. A slower, pricier one earns its keep on something that actually needs careful reasoning. There's no single best model for everything you do.

ROI: is your subscription actually worth it?

Estimate how many hours AI saved you this week, multiply by your hourly rate (or a fair estimate of what your time is worth), and compare that number to what you pay for it. If the gap is wide, it's earning its keep. If it's not, that's worth knowing too.

  • Disclosure norms vary by workplace and context. When in doubt, say what you used AI for
  • A model that's great at code can be mediocre at nuanced writing, and vice versa
  • Redo the ROI math every month or two. Your usage and your rates both change

Take the AI answer you got ready on the setup page. Paste it into your AI tool along with the prompt below.

Here's an answer I got from an AI tool: [paste the answer]. List the factual claims in this answer that I should independently verify, and suggest what kind of source would confirm each one (a specific study, an official statistic, a primary document, and so on).
Why this prompt works

Asking the model to separate out its own claims and suggest a verification source turns a vague "check this" into a concrete checklist. It also works regardless of which tool produced the original answer.

Pick at least one claim it flagged and actually look it up, using the kind of source it suggested. That's the step that catches a fabrication, not the flagging itself.

🔧 Troubleshooting: it just defends its own claims

Ask it to list the claims "without judging whether they're true." Separating listing from judging gets some models to hedge less.

Same pattern as the demo: the giveaway usually isn't tone. It's a specific-sounding detail (a citation, a date, an exact number) that nobody has actually checked.

Build 2 · Cost

Compare three models on the same task

Take one real task, something you'd actually ask AI to do, and run it on 3 or 4 different models. Notice what's different: which answer you'd actually trust, which came back fastest, which felt like overkill.

Here's the same request I gave to a few different AI models: [your task]. Their answers were: [Model A answer], [Model B answer], [Model C answer]. Compare them on speed, depth of reasoning, and how much you'd trust each one for this kind of task. Recommend which one I should default to for tasks like this.

Jot down what you notice, even a one-line note per model. That's the start of your own "which model for which task" reference, and it's worth more than any generic comparison chart because it's built on your actual work.

Build 3 · ROI

Calculate your real ROI

Take a real number. Estimate how many hours AI saved you this week, multiply by your hourly rate (or a fair estimate of what your time is worth), and compare that to what you pay for your AI tools this month. For example: 3 hours saved at $40 an hour is $120 of value, against a $20 monthly subscription, which is about $4.65 a week. That's a wide margin even if your hours estimate is generous.

Help me figure out whether my AI subscription is worth it. This week I estimate it saved me about [X] hours on [what kind of work]. My hourly rate is (or a fair estimate of my time's value is) $[Y] an hour. I pay $[Z] per month across my AI tools. Walk through the math for a weekly and monthly comparison, and flag if my hours-saved estimate seems generous or conservative.
